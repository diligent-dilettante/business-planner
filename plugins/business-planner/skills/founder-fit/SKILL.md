# Founder-Fit

A plan is only as good as the person running it. The same plan is a strong bet for one founder and a reckless one for another. This skill profiles who's actually executing and reshapes the plan around their real strengths, gaps, and network — making the **trade-offs explicit**: what to run confidently, what to de-risk, what to hand off.

**Tone: supportive but honest.** Name gaps plainly — vague encouragement helps no one — but always frame them as solvable through learning, hiring, or partnering. A gap is a design constraint, not a verdict on the founder.

**Core idea — the trade-off.** No founder is strong everywhere. Time and attention are finite. Every hour spent shoring up a weakness is an hour not spent compounding a strength. So the plan should deliberately decide, area by area: lean into strength, cheaply de-risk a gap, or buy/borrow the capability. Naming this beats pretending the founder will become great at everything.

---

## When invoked inside business-planner

Run after the bet and market are validated (post Track A §A3 / Track B §B2) but before or alongside go-to-market, ops, and execution — because those sections should be shaped by who's running them. Feed the output into A7/A10 (or B6/B10).

Can also run standalone: "assess my founder-market fit for X."

## The profile (interview, don't assume)

Ask conversationally across five dimensions. One or two questions each — this is a profile, not an interrogation. If two founders, profile both and note complementarity/overlap.

**1. Conviction & motivation.**
- Why this business, why you, why now? How long have you cared about this?
- Would you still do it if it stayed small? (Tests livelihood-vs-growth honesty.)
- What would make you quit? (Tests durability under the inevitable hard months.)
- *Reading it:* conviction predicts survival through the trough. Low conviction + high capital-at-risk is a flag. High conviction + no evidence is a different flag (see bias watch).

**2. Domain expertise (and adjacent).**
- How well do you know this industry — as an operator, a customer, or an outsider?
- What adjacent domains do you know that transfer (a restaurateur opening a food brand; a physiotherapist starting a padel club)?
- Where are the known unknowns — the parts you know you don't know?
- *Reading it:* deep domain fit lets a founder move fast and trust their judgment. An outsider can still win but must buy or borrow domain knowledge early, and should discount their own gut.

**3. Willingness to learn.**
- For the gaps: will you learn it, hire it, or partner for it? Realistically, given your time?
- What have you taught yourself before, and how did that go?
- *Reading it:* willingness-to-learn can substitute for expertise on non-critical-path items and slow-moving skills. It cannot substitute on the critical path under time pressure — there, buy it.

**4. Skillset — hard and soft.**
- Hard (resume): what can you actually do — build, sell, make, finance, operate, market, design?
- Soft: are you better at starting or finishing? Solo focus or people/relationships? Selling or building? Detail or vision?
- *Reading it:* map skills to what the business most needs *first*. A product genius who can't sell needs a distribution answer on day one, not year three.

**5. Network & unfair advantages.**
- Who do you already know who's a customer, supplier, hire, partner, or advisor?
- Distribution you already have (audience, community, reputation, existing customers)?
- Anything you can access that a stranger to this problem can't?
- *Reading it:* network is often the realest moat a small founder has. An existing audience or supplier relationship de-risks whole sections of the plan.

## The output — the Founder-Fit Map

A table mapping each major plan area to a decision. This is the deliverable.

| Plan area | Founder strength here? | Decision | Trade-off / note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product / making | [strong / mixed / gap] | **Run confidently** / De-risk / Get help | [what you gain or give up] |
| Sales / distribution | ... | ... | ... |
| Operations / supply chain | ... | ... | ... |
| Finance / numbers | ... | ... | ... |
| Marketing / brand | ... | ... | ... |
| Hiring / team | ... | ... | ... |
| Domain/regulatory knowledge | ... | ... | ... |

**Three decisions, defined:**
- **Run confidently** — real strength or strong fit. Lead here, move fast, trust your judgment. Don't over-hire into your own strength.
- **De-risk** — a gap that's learnable or non-critical-path. Set a cheap learning plan, a checklist, an advisor, or a reversible first step. Revisit before it becomes critical.
- **Get help** — a critical-path gap you can't close in time. Hire, partner, outsource, or bring a co-founder. Name the cost (money, equity, control, dependency) — that's the trade-off.

**Then: the one honest paragraph.** Given this profile, where is this founder genuinely advantaged, where are they exposed, and what's the single most important capability to secure (learn/hire/partner) before committing capital? Supportive but straight.

**Founder stories.** Turn the map into strict-template stories the plan is accountable to (feeds the Stories Ledger): one per major strength and at least one per critical gap.
> *As the founder, because [background/skill/network], I want to [own / lead X], so that [outcome], and I'd [run it confidently / hire / partner / budget ₹X].*
Strengths get `evidence` status where the founder has actually done it under real stakes; untested claims stay `assumed`.

## How this reshapes the plan (feed back into business-planner)

- **Go-to-market (A7/B6):** if the founder can't sell and has no distribution, a "we'll do D2C and figure out marketing" plan is fantasy. Force a distribution answer that fits their actual skills/network — or a hire.
- **Operations (A6/B5):** an outsider to the domain needs supplier/QC expertise bought in early; a domain veteran can run leaner.
- **Execution & hiring (A10/B10):** first hire should usually cover the founder's most critical gap, not duplicate their strength.
- **Financial plan (A9/B8):** "get help" decisions cost money or equity — put them in the numbers, don't hand-wave them.
- **Ambition calibration:** low conviction or high-gap + high-capital-at-risk argues for a smaller, more reversible first step.

## Bias & fallacy watch (specific to founder self-assessment)

- **Overconfidence / Dunning-Kruger** — founders overrate skills they've never tested at stake. *Q: where have you actually done this under real pressure, not in theory?*
- **Founder-as-customer** — "I'd buy it" ≠ the market will. Conviction is not evidence of demand. *Q: what proof exists outside your own enthusiasm?*
- **Hero bias** — believing grit alone closes any gap. Grit closes some gaps; on the critical path, under time pressure, buy the capability. *Q: is there time to learn this before it bites?*
- **Impostor underclaim** — the reverse: capable founders underrate real strengths and over-hire into them, diluting equity/control needlessly. *Q: is this actually a gap, or are you discounting a genuine strength?*
- **Network mirage** — "I know someone" isn't a commitment. *Q: have they actually said yes, or is it a hope?*

Hold both directions honestly: don't inflate the founder, don't crush them. Map reality, then design around it.
