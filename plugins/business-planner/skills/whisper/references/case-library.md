# Case Library — reference

Real precedent for physical businesses, structured to **separate transferable lessons from luck and context.** Each case: **what they did · why it worked then/there · transferable · luck/unrepeatable · who failed doing similar.** Figures are directional and dated — web-search to confirm specifics.

**Read this first:** these are winners (mostly), and winners are a biased sample. Use each case to generate a hypothesis to test in the founder's context — never as proof that a move works. Match to the founder's sector and ambition, not to fame.

---

## PART A — Indian icons (take the principle, discard the era)

### Amul (dairy cooperative, 1946→)
- **What they did:** three-tier farmer cooperative — village societies collect/test milk, district unions process, GCMMF markets. Removed middlemen; farmers own the profits. One master brand across 50+ products; low-cost witty advertising (the Amul Girl, since 1966).
- **Why it worked then/there:** post-independence rural India, farmers exploited by middlemen, no organised dairy — a trust and aggregation vacuum. Cooperative structure fit a low-trust, fragmented supplier base.
- **Transferable:** aggregate a fragmented market and give control/fair value to the supply side to earn loyalty; umbrella branding spreads trust and marketing cost cheaply; consistent quality builds the real moat.
- **Luck/unrepeatable:** a specific policy era (Operation Flood, government backing), a first-mover vacuum, a generational leader (Kurien). You won't get that clear a field.
- **Who failed doing similar:** most agricultural cooperatives in India never scaled — weak governance, politics, no distribution discipline. Cooperative structure alone guarantees nothing.

### Haldiram's (namkeen/snacks, 1937→)
- **What they did:** started as one Bikaner sweet shop; a differentiated bhujia recipe; expanded region by region (Bikaner → Nagpur/Kolkata/Delhi → national → 100+ export countries); family-run; now omnichannel with 400+ SKUs.
- **Why it worked then/there:** regional taste loyalty, a genuinely distinctive product, decades to compound before national FMCG competition arrived.
- **Transferable:** win one region completely before expanding; a distinctive product on taste/quality can carry a brand; patient regional-depth-first growth beats thin national spread.
- **Luck/unrepeatable:** 1937 start with a near-empty branded-snack field and 60+ years to build; a family recipe as moat. New entrants face crowded shelves and fast copying.
- **Who failed doing similar:** countless regional snack makers never crossed their home state — no quality consistency or distribution investment to travel.

### Nirma (detergent, 1969→)
- **What they did:** a low-cost detergent priced for the masses, sold where big brands (Surf) ignored — small towns and villages — via a dense distributor network; heavy jingle advertising built recall.
- **Why it worked then/there:** a huge underserved price-sensitive segment the premium incumbent priced out; a founder (Karsanbhai Patel) selling door-to-door first.
- **Transferable:** a large ignored price segment + a genuinely lower cost structure + distribution reach can beat an incumbent; start hand-to-hand, prove demand, then scale distribution.
- **Luck/unrepeatable:** a complacent premium monopoly leaving the bottom wide open; a pre-liberalisation market. Today's incumbents defend the value segment harder.
- **Who failed doing similar:** many cheap-detergent clones followed and died — low price without a real cost advantage or distribution just erodes margin.

### Modern Indian brands (recent — unproven over time, cite with care)
- **Mamaearth/Honasa** — D2C personal care, digital-first, influencer-led; listed, thin profits. *Lesson:* digital D2C + content can build a brand fast in India1/2. *Caveat:* profitability is fragile; ad-CAC rising; not a livelihood-business model.
- **boAt** — affordable-premium audio, design + marketing over manufacturing (outsourced). *Lesson:* win on brand/design/price in a category dominated by expensive incumbents. *Caveat:* wafer-thin hardware margins, import-dependent, brutal competition.
- **The Sleep Company / Sweet Karam Coffee / The Good Bug** — Fireside-backed premiumization plays (comfort, regional-premium F&B, gut health). *Lesson:* premium/functional upgrades of everyday categories ride India1's deepening. *Caveat:* all young; time will judge.

## PART A2 — Modern fast-growing brands (2020s — the current playbook, but young)

These show *today's* 0→1 physical playbook in India. Powerful and instructive — but recent, many unprofitable or pre-IPO, and fortunes change fast. Take the move, keep the caveat, and never assume the win is durable.

**The recurring modern playbook (what these share):**
- Founder solved a *personal, real frustration* in a broken/unorganised category.
- Digital-first discovery (Instagram/reels, micro-influencers) + phygital or q-comm distribution.
- A transparency / health / quality story that legacy FMCG couldn't credibly tell.
- Category with **built-in repeat** (food, grooming, meat, milk) beats one-off durables for retention.
- Rode the "3 Ps": Penetration (5G, Tier-2/3 smartphones), Platforms (ONDC, UPI, q-comm), Personalization.

**Cases:**
- **Lenskart (eyewear)** — reimagined the *experience* (virtual try-on, home eye tests, phygital stores), not just online sales. ~₹5,500 Cr revenue. *Transferable:* solve the whole customer problem, not just the transaction; phygital beats pure-online for considered/fitted goods. *Caveat:* capital-heavy store network; hard to copy at small scale.
- **Licious (fresh meat)** — built India's first organised cold-chain for a category full of "horror stories"; basics done exceptionally well = trust. First D2C meat unicorn, ~₹3,000 Cr+. *Transferable:* in a broken, low-trust, unorganised category, hygiene + reliability + cold chain *is* the differentiation. *Caveat:* brutal supply chain capex and wastage; years to profitability.
- **Country Delight (milk/grocery subscription)** — daily-consumable + subscription = structural repeat; transparency on sourcing. *Transferable:* daily-need + subscription is the strongest retention model in D2C; sell trust in what's *in* the product. *Caveat:* thin food margins (35–55%), logistics-heavy.
- **Sugar Cosmetics (BPC)** — beauty tailored to Indian skin tones; content + omnichannel. ~₹500 Cr. *Transferable:* localise for India-specific need a global brand ignores; omnichannel beats online-only in beauty. *Caveat:* crowded, ad-CAC intensive.
- **WickedGud / Storia / Sleepy Owl / Farmley (better-for-you F&B)** — high-protein/clean-label versions of everyday foods (pasta, beverages, coffee, dry-fruits/snacks), playful Gen-Z-first branding, digital + q-comm. *Transferable:* take a loved everyday category and make it cleaner/healthier with a shareable brand — the wellness-led premiumization wedge. *Caveat:* very young, mostly unprofitable, easily copied — the edge must be brand + distribution, not just recipe.
- **Minicult (kidswear)** — filled the gap between overpriced premium and low-quality cheap, built for Indian climate. *Transferable:* the "affordable-quality" middle is real white space when incumbents are barbelled at premium and junk. *Caveat:* apparel is working-capital-heavy with high returns.
- **Shark-Tank-India-era micro-brands** — hundreds of small founders reaching ₹1–10 Cr via a single hero product + Instagram + q-comm. *Transferable:* a focused hero SKU + social discovery + one channel can reach a livelihood-to-lower-growth outcome fast. *Caveat:* survivorship — for each visible one, many died; virality ≠ viable unit economics.

**The age angle across these:** most modern D2C winners targeted young, digital-first buyers — so discovery is social, values/transparency gate the sale, quality beats discount, and loyalty is fluid, and they engineer community/experience/subscription repeat. But note the sampling: this is where *venture-funded* action clustered, not the only place businesses are built. Millennial peak-earners, Gen X, and the under-served senior/silver economy are large, higher-margin, less-contested markets — a livelihood or growth business often does better aiming there than fighting for the same young buyers. Match the cohort to the wallet and the need. (See `india-consumption-by-age.md`.)

**Modern failure patterns (the other half):**
- Growth funded by discounting to unprofitable CAC — revenue up, cash gone.
- Viral spike mistaken for product-market fit; couldn't retain.
- Recipe/idea copied within months because the only "moat" was the product.
- Over-raised, over-scaled before margins worked (premature scaling, VC-funded edition).

## PART A3 — Modern Indian institutions (proven at scale — with today's caveats)

### DMart / Avenue Supermarts (value retail, 2002→)
- **What they did:** everyday-low-cost supermarkets built on brutal operating discipline: **own the real estate** (no rent drag), pay suppliers in days not months (buying leverage → lower COGS), ruthless SKU discipline (few slow movers), cluster-based expansion (dense in one region before the next), high inventory turnover. Slow store growth, consistently profitable — the anti-blitzscale.
- **Why it worked then/there:** price-first Indian middle-class households + fragmented kirana competition + patient promoter capital (Radhakishan Damani) that never needed venture-speed growth.
- **Transferable:** cash discipline *is* a moat — paying suppliers fast buys margin nobody can see; own-vs-rent changes unit economics permanently; cluster depth beats national spread; profitable-slow compounds past funded-fast.
- **Luck/unrepeatable:** promoter wealth enabled real-estate ownership from early on; entered before organised value retail was contested.
- **Today's caveat (verify before citing):** quick commerce is pulling DMart's urban convenience-seeking customers — even the best physical model faces channel disruption. A winner's playbook has a shelf life.

### Asian Paints (paints/home décor, 1942→)
- **What they did:** dominated for decades on **distribution + demand data** — direct-to-dealer (bypassing wholesaler layers) with ~10× the touchpoints of rivals, and daily SKU-level demand sensing (famously computerized in the 1970s, before it was normal anywhere) enabling forecast-driven production and near-JIT supply to tens of thousands of dealers.
- **Transferable:** the moat can live in *logistics and information*, not the product — same paint, radically better availability and freshness; invest in demand data absurdly early for your size.
- **Luck/unrepeatable:** decades of compounding before serious competition; category with strong repaint cycles.
- **Today's caveat (verify):** facing its first real assault in a generation (Birla Opus entry, margin pressure) — even 75-year moats get stormed when a deep-pocketed entrant decides to pay for share.

### Titan / Tanishq (jewellery, 1994→)
- **What they did:** organised, hallmarked, fixed-price jewellery in a **trust-scarce category** run on opaque pricing and purity doubt — transparent making-charges, purity guarantees (Karatmeter in-store), old-gold exchange, national brand + showroom experience. Converted unbranded-category distrust into a premium.
- **Transferable:** in any category where the customer fears being cheated (jewellery, used goods, repairs, health), *institutionalised trust* — verification, transparency, guarantees — is the product; the Tata name helped but the mechanisms (test the gold in front of the customer) are copyable at any scale.
- **Luck/unrepeatable:** Tata brand equity as a trust head-start; gold's cultural centrality (esp. South India — see regional reference).
- **Counter:** many branded-jewellery attempts failed by being *only* branded — premium price without the verification mechanisms that make trust tangible.

### Patanjali (FMCG, ~2006→, peak run 2014–17)
- **What they did (the rise):** swadeshi/ayurveda positioning + a celebrity-founder distribution of trust (Ramdev's following) + price undercutting of MNC FMCG + an existing channel (ayurvedic clinics/stores) — scaled to challenge HUL/Colgate in a few years with negligible ad-spend-per-rupee.
- **What happened (the stall — as instructive as the rise):** stretched the umbrella across too many categories too fast, quality-consistency failures, distribution debt (pushed stock without sell-through), and the trust asset eroding under scrutiny. Growth stalled sharply after 2017.
- **Transferable:** a genuine identity/values wedge + an owned audience can beat ad-budget incumbents *fast*; **and** the same case is the canonical umbrella-over-stretch + quality-drift failure pattern — both halves are the lesson.
- **Luck/unrepeatable:** a founder with a pre-built mass following is not a replicable input.

## PART B — Comparable economies (patterns transfer, specifics don't)

Businesses that scaled when their home market was near India's current stage — low-to-mid income, traditional-trade-dominant, price-sensitive, brand-hungry.

### Masan Consumer — Vietnam (sauces/FMCG)
- **What they did:** branded and standardised fish/table sauces, upgrading consumers from unbranded open-market product; grew Nam Ngu table-sauce share from ~2% (2005) to ~36% (2009).
- **Transferable:** in a category sold mostly loose/unbranded, being the first to brand + standardise + distribute can capture share fast. Look for large unbranded categories.
- **Luck/context:** a specific Vietnamese moment of rising incomes + trust shift; ≠ India's structure.
- **Counter:** branding a loose category only works where consumers will pay for trust/consistency — many attempts failed where loose was "good enough."

### Universal Robina (Philippines) & Mayora (Indonesia) — packaged foods
- **What they did:** low-cost positions + deep local taste knowledge to win snacks/confectionery against Western multinationals, then expand regionally ("new Asian multinationals").
- **Transferable:** low cost + local taste is the durable emerging-market edge multinationals can't easily copy; consolidate a category to raise entry barriers.
- **Luck/context:** regional expansion rode ASEAN integration; category-specific.
- **Counter:** low cost alone, without distinctive local taste or scale, just invites a price war.

### Sadia / Arisco / Parmalat-Brazil — umbrella branding
- **What they did:** leveraged one trusted brand across many food categories (milk → cookies → juices) to create scale cheaply.
- **Transferable:** umbrella branding is a proven emerging-market scale tactic where per-category ad spend is expensive relative to category size.
- **Luck/context:** Brazil's specific retail/media economics; works best with genuine cross-category trust.
- **Counter:** over-extended umbrellas dilute and confuse — stretching a brand into unrelated categories has sunk many.

### Unilever sachets — Africa/South Asia (small-pack)
- **What they did:** single-use sachets of shampoo, detergent, etc., at a few rupees, unlocking low-income consumers who can't buy full-size.
- **Transferable:** small-pack/sachet economics open a market a large SKU can't reach; match unit size to the customer's cash cycle.
- **Luck/context:** works for consumable, divisible goods; margin per sachet is thin — needs volume + distribution.
- **Counter:** sachets can trap you in low-margin volume and cannibalise larger packs if mispriced.

## PART C — The failure patterns (the other half of the truth)

Why comparable physical businesses *died* — as important as why they won:
- **Premature national/scale expansion** before the model or margins were proven (the #1 killer).
- **Low price without a real cost advantage** — a race to the bottom that starves the business.
- **No distribution investment** — great product that never reached the small store/shelf.
- **Umbrella over-stretch** — trust diluted across too many unrelated categories.
- **Copying a winner's visible move** while missing its hidden asset (Amul's policy backing, Haldiram's 60-year head start).
- **Trust never earned** — entering a trust-scarce market with no community, referral, or consistency engine.

## How to deliver a case (reminder)

Lead with the transferable principle → give the concrete example → **always** state the luck/context caveat and, where known, the counter-case → tie to the founder's specific next test. Match sector + ambition. When nothing fits, reason from the recurring patterns and say the analogy is imperfect. Never "do what they did."
