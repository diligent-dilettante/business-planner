# Book Library — extraction reference

Per book: **core bet · framework · do this · caveat/fit.** Crisp. Applied, not summarized. Honesty about scale and context is a feature — flag when a book is venture-only, dated, US-centric, or software-shaped, and translate to physical/local/any-scale.

---

## IDEA & VALIDATION

### The Mom Test — Rob Fitzpatrick
- **Core bet:** customer conversations are worthless if you ask about your idea; ask about their life and past behavior instead.
- **Framework:** talk about *their* problem, not your solution. Ask what they do now, what it costs them, when they last sought a fix. Good data = facts about the past, commitments (time/money/reputation), specifics. Compliments are worthless.
- **Do this:** interview 10 potential customers without pitching. Log what they currently spend/do. Ask for a small commitment (deposit, pre-order, intro). Count commitments, not smiles.
- **Fit:** universal, any scale, physical or digital. The single best book for demand validation. No caveats.

### The Lean Startup — Eric Ries
- **Core bet:** a startup is an experiment engine; success = validated learning, fastest at lowest cost.
- **Framework:** Build–Measure–Learn. MVP = smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption. Pivot = evidence-driven change of direction. Innovation accounting = measure learning, not vanity metrics.
- **Do this:** name your riskiest assumption; design the cheapest test; set in advance the result that means pivot; run it before building the full product.
- **Caveat/fit:** written for software where iteration is nearly free. For physical goods, "MVP" = a small batch, pop-up, pre-sale, or single stall — iteration costs real money, so test demand *before* tooling/inventory. Beware "MVP theater" (shipping something so minimal it tests nothing).

### Zero to One — Peter Thiel
- **Core bet:** great businesses create something new (0→1), they don't copy (1→n). Monopoly (own a market) beats competition.
- **Framework:** the contrarian question — "what important truth do few agree with you on?" Escape competition by being unique. Last-mover advantage: own a small market, then expand.
- **Do this:** define the small market you can dominate first. Name what you do that no one else does. If the honest answer is "the same but better/cheaper," rethink.
- **Caveat/fit:** venture-scale, software/tech framing, monopoly rhetoric. Translate: "monopoly" can be local — own one town, niche, or channel completely. Ignore the fundraising-scale ambition if you're building a livelihood; keep the differentiation discipline.

### Competing Against Luck (Jobs To Be Done) — Christensen
- **Core bet:** customers "hire" products to make progress in a situation; understand the job, not the demographics.
- **Framework:** the Job = the progress a customer seeks in a circumstance, including functional, emotional, social dimensions. Competitors = anything else that does the job (incl. doing nothing).
- **Do this:** write the job as "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." List every alternative that does that job. Design for the job.
- **Fit:** universal. Especially sharp for physical/local — reveals real competitors you'd otherwise miss.

### Nail It Then Scale It — Furr & Ahlstrom
- **Core bet:** validate the product AND business model (nail it) before spending to grow (scale it).
- **Framework:** nail the pain → nail the solution → nail the go-to-market → nail the model. Only then scale.
- **Do this:** don't spend on growth until you have repeatable demand and margins that work. Prove the loop small first.
- **Fit:** universal; directly counters premature scaling, the #1 killer.

## PRODUCT, HABIT, RETENTION

### Hooked — Nir Eyal
- **Core bet:** habit-forming products create a loop the customer returns to unprompted.
- **Framework:** Trigger → Action → Variable Reward → Investment. Each loop the user invests something that loads the next trigger.
- **Do this:** identify what brings a customer back. Build a real reason to return (consumable runs out, loyalty, subscription, service relationship). If it's one-time, accept you live on constant new acquisition.
- **Caveat/fit:** written for apps; the loop for physical is retention/repeat-purchase, not screen addiction. Eyal's own ethics test: if the loop only benefits you, it's a dark pattern and churns. Don't manipulate.

### The E-Myth Revisited — Michael Gerber
- **Core bet:** most small businesses fail because the owner is a technician who never builds a system; they own a job, not a business.
- **Framework:** work ON the business not just IN it. Build it as if you'll franchise it — documented systems so it runs without you.
- **Do this:** document your core processes (how the product is made, sold, delivered). Make yourself replaceable in at least one role this quarter.
- **Fit:** essential for livelihood/MSME owners. The antidote to burnout-by-self-employment. Timeless.

## POSITIONING & MOAT

### Obviously Awesome (Positioning) — April Dunford
- **Core bet:** customers understand you by contrast; weak positioning kills good products.
- **Framework:** 5 (+1) components — competitive alternatives → unique attributes → the value those enable → who cares most (best-fit customers) → market category (the context that makes your value obvious) → (trend, optionally).
- **Do this:** write what the customer would use if you didn't exist. State your unique attributes, the value they create, and the category you want to be understood in. Test the pitch against that alternative.
- **Fit:** universal, any scale. The clearest positioning method available.

### Seven Powers — Hamilton Helmer
- **Core bet:** only a few advantages are truly durable; "we try harder" isn't one.
- **Framework:** scale economies, network effects, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resource, process power.
- **Do this:** identify which (if any) power you're building. For local business, brand/trust, cornered resource (location, exclusive supplier), and switching costs are the realistic ones.
- **Caveat/fit:** dense, strategy-heavy. Use the taxonomy as a checklist; skip the math unless you love it.

### Blue Ocean Strategy — Kim & Mauborgne
- **Core bet:** stop fighting in bloody "red oceans"; create uncontested space.
- **Framework:** Eliminate–Reduce–Raise–Create grid — change which factors you compete on so rivals become irrelevant.
- **Do this:** list what your industry takes for granted. What could you eliminate or invert to serve customers differently?
- **Caveat/fit:** examples are big-corporate; the ERRC grid works at any scale. Beware over-claiming a "blue ocean" that's actually empty because there's no demand.

## GROWTH & DISTRIBUTION

### Traction — Weinberg & Mares
- **Core bet:** most businesses die from no distribution, not no product; find your one channel systematically.
- **Framework:** 19 channels + the Bullseye — brainstorm all, cheaply test the promising few, focus on the one that works.
- **Do this:** list every possible channel. Run cheap tests on three. Kill the losers, pour into the winner.
- **Fit:** universal. Directly counters spray-and-pray.

### $100M Offers / $100M Leads — Alex Hormozi
- **Core bet:** a good enough offer removes price resistance; then flood it with leads.
- **Framework:** Value = (dream outcome × perceived likelihood) ÷ (time delay × effort). Raise value and cut risk (guarantees) instead of cutting price.
- **Do this:** rebuild your offer to increase perceived likelihood and reduce customer risk/effort. Add a guarantee. Only then scale lead-gen.
- **Caveat/fit:** loud, self-promotional tone; the offer math is genuinely useful and very applicable to D2C/local/service. Ignore the hustle theater, keep the value equation.

## MONEY & SURVIVAL

### Profit First — Mike Michalowicz
- **Core bet:** businesses that pay expenses first never have profit left; flip it.
- **Framework:** Revenue − Profit = Expenses. Allocate profit (and tax, owner's pay) to separate accounts first; run the business on what's left.
- **Do this:** open separate accounts; take a fixed profit % off every deposit before spending. Force the business to fit its real means.
- **Fit:** excellent for livelihood/MSME cash discipline. Simple, behavioral, works.

### The Intelligent Investor — Benjamin Graham (for the owner-as-capital-allocator)
- **Core bet:** deploy capital only above its next-best use; margin of safety.
- **Framework:** cost of capital / return hurdle — any money (or time) in the business must beat what it could earn elsewhere (deposit, index, debt paydown).
- **Do this:** compute your return on invested capital including your own time. Below the hurdle → it's a hobby, not a business.
- **Caveat/fit:** it's an investing book; borrow only the hurdle-rate discipline. This is the skill's core definition of "a business."

## PEOPLE, EXECUTION, DECISIONS

### The Hard Thing About Hard Things — Ben Horowitz
- **Core bet:** the hard part has no formula; it's leading through problems with no good options.
- **Framework:** take care of people, product, profits — in that order. Face brutal facts early. There are no silver bullets, only lead bullets (do the hard work).
- **Do this:** name the hard truth you're avoiding and act on it this week.
- **Caveat/fit:** venture-CEO war stories; mindset transfers, specifics (board fights, layoffs at scale) may not. Keep the candor.

### Who — Geoff Smart & Randy Street
- **Core bet:** hiring mistakes cause most business pain; hire on evidence of outcomes.
- **Framework:** scorecard (define the role's outcomes) → source → structured interview → sell. Hire "A players" against the scorecard, not on charm.
- **Do this:** before any hire, write the 3–5 outcomes that define success. Interview for evidence of each.
- **Fit:** universal once you hire anyone. High leverage, under-used by small businesses.

### Good to Great — Jim Collins
- **Core bet:** great companies get the right people first, confront reality, and focus on one thing.
- **Framework:** First Who Then What; the Hedgehog Concept (best at × drives economics × passionate about); confront the brutal facts.
- **Do this:** find the intersection of what you can be best at and what makes money — do that, drop the rest.
- **Caveat/fit:** big-company study, some examples aged badly; the Hedgehog and First-Who ideas hold up.

### The One Thing — Gary Keller
- **Core bet:** extraordinary results come from narrow focus, not doing more.
- **Framework:** the Focusing Question — "what's the ONE thing I can do such that everything else becomes easier or unnecessary?"
- **Do this:** each week, name the one domino. Do it first, before anything else.
- **Fit:** universal, especially for overloaded solo owners.

### Thinking, Fast and Slow — Kahneman  ·  Predictably Irrational — Ariely
- **Core bet:** human judgment is systematically biased; know the traps.
- **Framework (use as checklist):** anchoring, availability, overconfidence, loss aversion, sunk cost, confirmation, planning fallacy.
- **Do this:** run big decisions through the bias checklist; force a written worst-case; get an outside view.
- **Fit:** universal. The source material for the skill's bias engine. Dense — use the list, skip the full read unless curious.

## SMALL / LEAN BY CHOICE (validates the livelihood profile)

### The $100 Startup — Chris Guillebeau
- **Core bet:** many great businesses start cheap, solve a real need, and never need scale or funding.
- **Do this:** find the overlap of what you can offer and what people pay for; sell before you build; keep overhead near zero.
- **Fit:** perfect for livelihood profile. Antidote to scale bias.

### The 4-Hour Workweek — Tim Ferriss
- **Core bet:** design the business around the life you want; automate and eliminate.
- **Do this:** define the income and time you actually want; build to that, not to "bigger." Test demand before building; automate fulfilment.
- **Caveat/fit:** dated tactics (some 2007-era), lifestyle branding; the "define your own target, don't default to scale" principle is the keeper.

### Company of One — Paul Jarvis
- **Core bet:** growth isn't always the answer; staying small can be the strategy.
- **Do this:** ask what problems growth would actually solve before pursuing it; optimize for resilience and margin over size.
- **Fit:** the clearest articulation of "small on purpose." Direct counter to scale bias.

## FUNDRAISING (only for venture profile — never push on livelihood owners)

### Venture Deals — Feld & Mendelson
- **Core bet:** understand term sheets before you sign; know what actually matters (control + economics).
- **Do this:** learn the terms (liquidation preference, board, vesting, pro-rata) before raising. Only relevant if you're taking VC.
- **Fit:** venture only. Skip entirely for self-funded / loan / bootstrapped businesses.

## HONEST OVERALL NOTES
- The "startup canon" (Lean Startup, Zero to One) is venture-software-shaped. Spirit transfers, specifics often don't — translate to physical/local.
- Motivation-heavy classics (Think and Grow Rich, Rich Dad Poor Dad) are fine for mindset, thin on method. Don't oversell.
- Fame ≠ fit. A local baker gets more from E-Myth + Profit First + The Mom Test than from Zero to One + Venture Deals.
- If a book isn't here, research it and apply the same structure: core bet · framework · do this · caveat/fit.

## INDIA & CONTRARIAN ADDITIONS

### The Fortune at the Bottom of the Pyramid — C.K. Prahalad
- **Core bet:** the world's low-income majority is a viable, profitable market — if you redesign the *offering* (unit size, price architecture, distribution, trust), not just discount the rich-market product.
- **Framework:** single-serve/sachet economics, radical affordability via redesign (not de-featuring), distribution through community networks, building consumption capacity.
- **Do this:** if targeting India2/3, redesign the unit — pack size to the customer's cash cycle, price to daily wallet, distribution to where they already buy. Never just shrink the premium product.
- **Caveat/fit:** written 2004; some celebrated cases aged badly and critics showed several "BoP wins" actually served the middle class. Use with the planner's India1/2/3 lens: CAC>LTV in true India3 is still the default — this book is the playbook *if* you have a specific reason to defy it.

### The Dhandho Investor — Mohnish Pabrai
- **Core bet:** "heads I win, tails I don't lose much" — the Patel-motel method: enter proven, boring businesses with tiny downside and real upside; minimise risk, not effort.
- **Framework:** buy/copy distressed or unfashionable proven models; low capex entry; few bets, big bets, infrequent bets; margin of safety on the *downside* first.
- **Do this:** before any novel idea, price the boring proven alternative (the existing shop for sale, the established franchise, the known trade). If the novel idea can't beat the boring one risk-adjusted, take the boring one.
- **Caveat/fit:** an investor's book, translated here to founder capital-allocation. The purest articulation of this skill's own cost-of-capital + kill-screen ethos — and the antidote to novelty bias.

### The Goal — Eliyahu Goldratt
- **Core bet:** every system has exactly one constraint at a time; improving anything except the constraint is an illusion of progress.
- **Framework:** Theory of Constraints — identify the bottleneck → exploit it fully → subordinate everything else to it → elevate it → repeat. Throughput > local efficiency.
- **Do this:** in any operations section, name the single constraint (the oven, the one machinist, the delivery van, the founder's hours). Measure it. Check every planned investment against "does this raise the constraint's throughput?" — most don't.
- **Caveat/fit:** written as a factory novel; applies to any physical business and to the founder's own time (often *the* constraint in a livelihood business). Skip the novel, keep the five steps.
