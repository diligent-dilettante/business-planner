# Business Frameworks — working reference

Distilled, physical-business-calibrated. Each entry: the framework, the one question it forces, and the trap it guards against. Use these inside grilling — cite the framework by name when it fires so the owner learns the lens, not just the answer.

Ambition-neutral note: most of these were written for venture software. Where a framework assumes scale or fundraising, the "physical / any-scale" line says how to use it for a livelihood or local business instead.

---

## Idea & problem

**Zero to One (Thiel) — the contrarian question.**
"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" A great business does something no one else does, not a slightly better version of what exists. Monopoly (own a small market completely) beats competition (fight in a crowded one).
- *Forces:* is this a genuinely different offer, or a marginally better copy?
- *Trap:* competing on being 10% better — a race to the bottom on price.
- *Physical / any-scale:* "monopoly" can be local. Own one town, one niche, one channel completely before widening. A bakery that owns its neighborhood is a monopoly.

**The Mom Test (Fitzpatrick) — how to validate demand honestly.**
Never ask "would you buy this?" People lie to be nice. Ask about their past behavior and current spending: what do you do today, what does it cost you, when did you last look for a fix. Talk about their life, not your idea.
- *Forces:* real evidence of demand vs polite encouragement.
- *Trap:* mistaking compliments for validation.
- *Physical / any-scale:* pre-orders, deposits, a waitlist with money down, or one paying pilot customer beat 100 "sounds great" replies.

**Jobs To Be Done (Christensen) — what the customer is really hiring you for.**
People don't buy products, they "hire" them to make progress in a situation. The milkshake isn't competing with other milkshakes — it's competing with bagels and boredom on a commute.
- *Forces:* what job, in what situation, is the customer hiring this for?
- *Trap:* defining competitors too narrowly (only direct rivals) and missing the real alternative.

**Start With Why (Sinek) — purpose as positioning.**
People buy why you do it, not just what you make. A clear why attracts the right customers and the right team.
- *Forces:* can you state why this exists beyond making money?
- *Trap:* leading with features; useful but secondary to demand and economics — don't let "why" excuse weak unit economics.

## Market & validation

**Lean Startup (Ries) — build-measure-learn, MVP, validated learning.**
Ship the smallest thing that tests your riskiest assumption. Learn, then iterate. A "pivot" is a structured change of direction based on evidence, not failure.
- *Forces:* what's the smallest test that proves or kills the riskiest assumption — and what's the result that would make you pivot?
- *Trap:* building the whole thing before testing demand; also "MVP theater" — shipping something so minimal it tests nothing.
- *Physical / any-scale:* MVP for physical = a small first batch, a single market stall, a pop-up, a pre-sale. Test before you tool up or commit inventory.

**Nail It Then Scale It (Furr) — sequence discipline.**
Get the product and business model right (nail it) before pouring fuel on growth (scale it). Scaling an unvalidated model just loses money faster.
- *Forces:* have you nailed repeatable demand and margins before spending to scale?
- *Trap:* premature scaling — the #1 startup killer.

**Crossing the Chasm (Moore) — early adopters ≠ mainstream.**
The people who love your rough early product are not the mainstream market. The gap between them is where many businesses die. Win one beachhead segment completely before broadening.
- *Forces:* who's your single beachhead segment, and are you mistaking enthusiasts for the mass market?
- *Trap:* assuming early traction generalizes.

**Bottom-up sizing (not TAM theater).**
Real size = reachable buyers × price × frequency, built from the ground up. Top-down "1% of a $10B market" is fiction.
- *Forces:* show the math from units and buyers.
- *Trap:* TAM inflation to look ambitious.

## Product, habit & retention

**Hooked (Eyal) — the habit loop (trigger → action → variable reward → investment).**
Products that stick create a loop the user returns to. Each cycle the user invests something (data, content, effort) that makes the next cycle more likely.
- *Forces:* what brings the customer back — a real loop, or a one-time sale?
- *Trap:* assuming repeat purchase without a mechanism for it.
- *Physical / any-scale:* the loop is retention: consumables that run out, a loyalty reason to return, a subscription, a service relationship. One-time-purchase businesses must win on constant new acquisition — know which you are.
- *Ethics flag (Eyal's own caveat):* habit-forming ≠ manipulation. If the loop only benefits you, not the customer, it's a dark pattern and it'll churn.

**The E-Myth Revisited (Gerber) — work ON the business, not just IN it.**
Most small businesses fail because the owner is a technician (great baker) who never builds the system (the bakery that runs without them). Systematize so it doesn't depend on you doing everything.
- *Forces:* does this business only work if you personally do the work forever?
- *Trap:* buying yourself a job instead of building a business. Critical for livelihood-profile owners.
- *Physical / any-scale:* document the recipe, the process, the supplier list — so it can run, scale, or be sold.

## Competition & positioning

**Positioning (Dunford) — position against the alternative, in a category the customer gets.**
Customers understand you by contrast. Define: the real alternative they'd otherwise pick, your unique attributes, the value those enable, who cares most, and the market category you slot into.
- *Forces:* what would the customer do if you didn't exist, and why is your slot better?
- *Trap:* positioning in a vacuum; "best quality" with no reference point.

**Seven Powers / moats (Helmer, Thiel) — what actually defends a business.**
Durable advantages: scale economies, network effects, switching costs, brand, cornered resource (exclusive supplier/location), process power. "We work harder" is not a moat.
- *Forces:* what's structural here that a funded copycat can't buy in a year?
- *Trap:* mistaking effort or taste for defensibility.
- *Physical / any-scale:* for local businesses the moat is often location, a supplier relationship, or brand/trust in a community — real and defensible even at small scale.

**Blue Ocean (Kim & Mauborgne) — compete where no one else is.**
Instead of fighting in a bloody "red ocean," create uncontested space by changing what you compete on (eliminate-reduce-raise-create).
- *Forces:* which industry norms could you drop or invert to make competition irrelevant?
- *Trap:* copying competitors' feature checklist and competing only on price.

## Growth & distribution

**Traction / Bullseye (Weinberg & Mares) — pick one channel, test systematically.**
There are ~19 acquisition channels. Most businesses win with one or two. Brainstorm all, cheaply test the promising few, double down on what works.
- *Forces:* the ONE channel you'll win first — not five you dabble in.
- *Trap:* spray-and-pray marketing; assuming "we'll do social + ads + SEO + retail" all at once.

**$100M Offers / Leads (Hormozi) — make the offer so good it's a no-brainer, then get leads flowing.**
Value = (dream outcome × likelihood) ÷ (time × effort). Increase perceived value and reduce risk (guarantees) rather than cutting price.
- *Forces:* is the offer itself compelling, or are you just discounting?
- *Trap:* price-cutting as the only lever.
- *Physical / any-scale:* very applicable to local/D2C — guarantees, bundles, and framing beat racing competitors down on price.

**Growth loops > funnels.**
A loop reinvests output into input (each customer brings the next: referral, content, marketplace supply). Funnels leak; loops compound.
- *Forces:* what compounds without linear ad spend?
- *Trap:* growth = more spend forever.

## Execution, people, decisions

**The Hard Thing About Hard Things (Horowitz) — there's no formula for the hard stuff.**
Real building is managing through problems that have no clean answer. Take care of the people, the product, and the profits — in that order. Face brutal facts early.
- *Forces:* what's the hard truth you're avoiding?
- *Trap:* wishful management; hoping problems resolve themselves.

**Who (Smart & Street) — hiring is the highest-leverage decision.**
Most business pain traces to a hiring mistake. Define the role's outcomes first (a scorecard), then hire for evidence of those outcomes, not charm.
- *Forces:* for any hire, what specific outcomes define success?
- *Trap:* hiring on gut/likability; the #1 avoidable business error.

**Good to Great (Collins) — get the right people, confront reality, find your one thing.**
"First who, then what." The Hedgehog Concept: the intersection of what you're best at, what drives your economics, and what you're passionate about.
- *Forces:* what's the single thing you can be best at that also makes money?
- *Trap:* diversifying before you've won one thing.

**The 4-Hour Workweek / $100 Startup (Ferriss / Guillebeau) — small, lean, profitable is a valid goal.**
Not every business should scale. A lean, automated, cash-flowing business that funds the life you want is a complete success. Start cheap, sell before you build, keep overhead low.
- *Forces:* does this need to be big, or does it need to be profitable and yours?
- *Trap:* scale bias — assuming bigger is always the goal. Explicitly validates the livelihood profile.

**The One Thing (Keller) — focus beats hustle.**
"What's the one thing such that by doing it everything else becomes easier or unnecessary?" Sequential focus over scattered effort.
- *Forces:* what's the one domino this month?
- *Trap:* busywork that feels productive but moves nothing.

## Money & survival

**Profit First (Michalowicz) — take profit off the top.**
Allocate profit before expenses (Revenue − Profit = Expenses), not after. Forces the business to be profitable by design and prevents the "grow revenue, never see money" trap.
- *Forces:* is this profitable by design, or hoping profit is left over?
- *Trap:* revenue vanity with no take-home.
- *Physical / any-scale:* especially powerful for livelihood/MSME owners drowning in top-line-but-no-cash.

**Cost of capital / return hurdle (value investing — Graham, Buffett).**
Any capital deployed should return more than its next-best use (a bank deposit, index fund, paying down debt). Below that hurdle, it's not a business — it's an expensive hobby.
- *Forces:* does the return beat your cost of capital, including your own time?
- *Trap:* mistaking revenue or busyness for a real return. This is the skill's core definition of a business.

**Runway & cash timing.**
Businesses die of cash, not lack of profit-on-paper. Model when cash actually arrives vs when bills are due. Inventory and receivables lock up cash.
- *Forces:* can you cover the gap between paying suppliers and getting paid?
- *Trap:* confusing profit with cash in the bank.

## Strategy frameworks (for the Strategy Doc — apply only where they bite)

- **Rumelt — Good Strategy Bad Strategy (the strategy doc's spine).** Strategy = kernel: **Diagnosis** (the critical factors) → **Guiding policy** (the approach) → **Coherent actions** (mutually reinforcing moves). Bad strategy = goals, buzzwords, or wishful thinking dressed up. *Trap:* mistaking ambition ("₹10cr in 3 years") for strategy.
- **Porter — Five Forces + generic strategies.** Industry attractiveness via rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power; then choose cost leadership, differentiation, or focus — being stuck in the middle loses. *Use:* competition sections; picking the slot. *Trap:* static analysis of a dynamic market.
- **PESTLE.** Political/Economic/Social/Tech/Legal/Environmental scan — in India: GST changes, state policy, festival economy, vernacular shift, compliance. *Use:* scenario inputs, not a ritual checklist.
- **Ansoff matrix.** Growth directions: existing/new product × existing/new market. Risk rises with distance from the core — Track B expansions should name their quadrant and the added risk. *Trap:* diagonal jumps (new product AND new market) disguised as adjacency.
- **Game-theory basics — reaction functions.** A competitor's response depends on *their* payoffs and constraints, not your hopes; credible commitments (sunk investment, public price promises) shape rivals' behavior; most small-entrant moves meet **indifference** until a threshold is crossed. *Use:* competition-sim.

## Operating frameworks (for the Operating Plan)

- **OKR-lite.** Max 3 outcome-phrased objectives, 1–3 measurable key results each, tied to plan numbers. *Trap:* activity lists dressed as outcomes; 10 objectives = none.
- **Theory of Constraints (Goldratt).** At any moment one bottleneck governs throughput — find it, exploit it, elevate it, repeat. The operating plan's center of gravity is the current constraint. *Trap:* optimizing non-bottlenecks feels productive and changes nothing.
- **Cadence design.** Daily glance (cash/orders) · weekly numbers-vs-plan (30 min, one decision) · monthly tripwire + stories review · quarterly strategy/scenario refresh. The rhythm, not the documents, is what keeps a plan alive.
- **Scenario planning + tripwires.** Named futures (base/squeeze/breakout/kill), leading indicators, pre-committed responses. Handles time honestly — prediction doesn't. *Trap:* scenarios written once and never reviewed.

## Cognitive biases (Kahneman, Ariely, Tsipursky) — the ones that kill businesses

Name it, one-line risk, correcting question. Fire these during grilling.

- **Planning fallacy** — everything takes longer and costs more. *Correcting Q: what's your estimate × 1.5, and can you survive that?*
- **Confirmation bias** — you seek evidence you're right. *Q: what did you look for that would prove you wrong?*
- **Sunk cost fallacy** — throwing good money after bad because you've already spent. *Q: if you were deciding fresh today, would you still start?*
- **Optimism/overconfidence** — no worst case modeled. *Q: what's the realistic worst case and do you survive it?*
- **Survivorship bias** — copying visible winners, ignoring the graveyard. *Q: how many who tried this failed, and why?*
- **Anchoring** — first number sticks (usually your cost, or a competitor's price). *Q: what would you charge if you'd never seen that anchor?*
- **Narrative fallacy** — a good story feels like proof. *Q: strip the story — what's the number?*
- **Availability bias** — one vivid example (a viral brand) drives the plan. *Q: is that typical or the rare exception?*
- **Scale bias** (skill-specific) — assuming venture-scale is the only valid outcome. *Q: what does success actually look like for you?*
- **Lifestyle dismissal** (skill-specific) — underplanning a small/livelihood business as "just a side thing." *Q: does a livelihood plan still need real unit economics? (yes.)*
- **Youth-default** (skill-specific) — assuming the young are the market; ignoring peak-earning millennials and under-served Gen X/seniors. *Q: which cohort has the wallet and the need — not the trend?*
- **Authority/novelty bias** — "an expert said" or "it's the new thing" as a substitute for evidence. *Q: does the evidence hold without the authority or the hype?*

## Sequencing (how these stack)

1. **Idea:** Mom Test + JTBD + Zero to One → is there a real, differentiated job worth doing?
2. **Validate:** Lean MVP → cheapest test of the riskiest assumption.
3. **Economics:** cost of capital + Profit First → does one unit/customer return above the hurdle?
4. **Nail it:** repeatable demand + margins before spend.
5. **Position:** Dunford + moat → why you, defensibly.
6. **One channel:** Bullseye → win one distribution path.
7. **Then scale:** growth loops + guardrails → compound without buying every customer.
8. **Systematize:** E-Myth + Who → build the machine, hire for outcomes.
Throughout: bias checks + worst-case survival.
